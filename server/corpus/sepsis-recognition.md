# Overview

This is a clearly-labelled reference summary of sepsis recognition adapted from
NICE guideline NG51 and NEWS2 escalation practice. It is intended for clinical
decision support and does not replace local protocol or clinician judgement.
Sepsis is life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host
response to infection. Early recognition and treatment reduce mortality.

# Risk Stratification

Assess anyone with suspected infection for signs of sepsis. Stratify risk using
physiology, NEWS2, and clinical context. Treat the following as HIGH RISK:
responsive only to voice or pain or unresponsive (ACVPU); systolic blood pressure
90 mmHg or lower, or a drop of more than 40 mmHg from baseline; heart rate over
130 per minute; respiratory rate 25 per minute or higher; needing oxygen to keep
SpO2 at or above 92 percent; non-blanching rash or mottled/ashen appearance;
lactate over 2 mmol/L; reduced urine output (below 0.5 mL/kg/hour); or recent
chemotherapy. A NEWS2 aggregate score of 5 or more, or 3 in any single parameter,
should trigger urgent senior clinical review.

# The Sepsis Six Bundle

For any patient meeting high-risk criteria, deliver the Sepsis Six within one hour
of recognition. The three "give" interventions are: give high-flow oxygen titrated
to target saturations; give intravenous broad-spectrum antibiotics per local
antimicrobial policy; and give an intravenous fluid bolus (balanced crystalloid,
typically 500 mL over 15 minutes, reassessing after each bolus). The three "take"
interventions are: take blood cultures before antibiotics where this does not delay
them; take a venous or arterial lactate; and take (measure) urine output, using a
catheter where appropriate. Escalate to critical care if lactate remains above
2 mmol/L after fluids, or the patient stays hypotensive.

# Escalation and Reassessment

Reassess the patient at least hourly. Seek senior review if there is no improvement
within one hour of starting treatment, if lactate fails to clear, or if the patient
needs vasopressors to maintain a mean arterial pressure of at least 65 mmHg. Document
the time of antibiotic administration; each hour of delay in antibiotics for septic
shock is associated with a measurable increase in mortality. Consider source control
(drainage, debridement, device removal) early.
